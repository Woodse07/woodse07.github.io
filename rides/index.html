How it works

Every line is a ride I actually rode. There are 212 of them, recorded on a Garmin and pulled out of Strava — a little over 4,584 km between August 2024 and July 2026, almost all of it within an hour of Dublin, with the rest picked up on trips to Vancouver, Mallorca, Rimini and Amsterdam.

The heat is done with compositing rather than with counting. Each track is stroked in translucent ink set to multiply, so paper crossed once stays pale and paper crossed two hundred times drives down to a deep burnt red. The stroke stays the same width on screen at every zoom level, which is the whole trick: zoom in and routes that merged into one dark line separate back into the individual roads that make it up.

Almost everything you can see is the tracks themselves — the climbs through the Wicklow Gap, the grid of streets I ride to get out of the city, all of it drawn only from where the bike has been. The one thing that is not is the faint grey shoreline, traced from OpenStreetMap, which is there because a heatmap floating in white space gives you nothing to hang it on. It was baked into a file when this page was built, so there is still no tile server, no API key and no mapping library, and nothing about your visit is sent anywhere to render it. Positions are projected equirectangular, with longitudes squeezed by the cosine of the view latitude, the same approach as the train map.

Turbo sessions are left out. Zwift reports positions in its own world, which lands off the coast of New York, and plotting them would smear a continent I have never cycled across the map.

A note on the missing bit

Rides that start at a front door draw a map with the front door at the centre of it, so the streets nearest home have been taken out before the data ever reached this page.

The first attempt cut every ride at the same 500 m, which turned out to be worse than useless: two hundred rides all stopping at exactly that distance drew a clean circle, and a circle has an obvious centre. So the cut is no longer a fixed distance. Each ride is given its own radius, then has a further random stretch of road trimmed off whichever end the cut fell on, so tracks now stop anywhere between about 700 m and three kilometres out, on whichever street that ride happened to leave by. The edge is ragged and the hole is lopsided rather than centred on anything. The coordinate being masked is not in the file the page loads.

Being straight about the limit: a rider who starts from home every day cannot publish a heatmap that hides the neighbourhood, because the neighbourhood is where all the lines converge. What this does is remove the street-level detail and take away the tidy geometric pointer. It makes the address hard to read off the map. It does not make the general area a secret.
